McDonald's got more profitable last year. The reason isn't what most people would guess.

Its international business simply grew into a bigger slice of the company. A much smaller licensing business became dramatically more profitable per dollar. The U.S., its biggest and most familiar market, actually held margin back.

What actually moved the margin

International: bigger, not better
+0.94pt
This segment's own profitability barely changed. What changed is that it now makes up more of McDonald's total revenue than it used to, 50.7% versus 48.7% a year earlier, and it happens to be a healthier-margin business than the U.S.
Licensing: better, not bigger
+0.64pt
This is the smaller segment covering licensed and developmental markets. Its share of revenue actually shrank, but its profit margin rose from 1.2% to 8.4%, nearly seven times higher.
The U.S.: neither
-0.51pt
McDonald's home market lost ground on both counts, a slightly smaller share of total revenue, and a slightly thinner margin.
Everything else
-0.15pt
Small offsetting moves in the other two segments, roughly canceling out.

Why it matters

People often describe McDonald's as a real estate and licensing company that happens to sell burgers. This is the first time that idea gets an actual number attached to it.

Just over half of last year's margin improvement came from the business getting bigger in a market that was already more profitable, not from anyone running restaurants better. Those are different things to bet on continuing, and the difference is the kind of thing an investor or a hiring manager would actually want pointed out, not buried in a spreadsheet.
